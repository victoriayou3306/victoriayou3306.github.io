The 2026 ASPE Student Challenge was to design a nanoindenter using modular Mech Blocks and a leaf-spring mechanism to determine material properties like hardness and elastic modulus. We were given a Berkovich Indentor, Capacitance Probe, Voice Coil Actuator, and Micrometer stage. We also had Cu substrates to test our indenter before the competition to determine a mystery sample's material. A basic LabView control dashboard was provided for actuation and measurement, and we had to build the indentation control loop around it. The Oliver-Pharr method was used for data analysis.

Justin and I originally met at our summer internship at KLA, and Vy Tran from the Mechatronics Team introduced us to ASPE. This conference was incredibly informative and fun, even though pretty much everything went wrong...(see last section), and we were able to meet so many incredible people. The community was unbelievably patient with us, and we are extremely grateful to Vy, the KLA team, and the whole ASPE board and society.

Mechanical Design

Learning about flexure designs was really interesting, and to be honest, it took a while for us to even understand the project principles. We started a few CAD designs, thinking about minimizing the moving mass to reduce deflection due to gravity, as well as lowering the resonance frequency. We were given 10 leaf springs to use, but wanted to minimize their usage because a. we would probably bend and damage a few, and b. the notion that the indentation force being a larger percentage of our actuation force (compared to force in the flexure mechanism) would improve the precision of our measurements.

Our very first design was unideal... but it was a start. We also realized that it was rather difficult to align the leaf springs and hardware perfectly, as even small deviations would cause large unexpected nonlinearities. There's also some quirks with the mech blocks, as each hole in the grid can only be utilized in one cartesian direction. We thought a horizontal orientation would be a little simpler to start with, but were a little concerned about the torque imbalance by our large (to be reduced) moving mass.

We met with some professors for some feedback and other leaf spring designs and flexure mechanisms. We decided to try a Double Leaf Spring design for complete linearity along the axis of indentation, which the parallelogram (v1) design would not have.

So then the mechanism was assembled, and set up to calibrate the capacitance probe with the micrometer stage. The moving stage ended up heavier than expected to include enough attachment points for both the actuator and indenter.

...And then I came back after class to do the calibration and it was sagging and misaligned due to the weight, so we had to rethink the design.

We started entertaining the idea of a vertical orientation, so the sagging doesn't produce a torque and offset the axis of indentation. But that would only work if the center of mass was inline with the plane of the leaf springs (right picture), and also becomes very bulky with the placement of the micrometer stage.

We realized the slight nonlinear flex of the parallelogram would be negligible at our scale of operation, and decided to go back to a revision of that design to continue with hardware calibration.

Hardware Calibration

We planned to start the calibration process in three steps: finding the capacitance probe gain (µm/V) and baseline error, finding the most linear region of voice coil operation, and the proportionality of Force vs Current/Input Voltage.

The ASPE committee gave us this dashboard to control the hardware. I added a 4s average & stdev readout of the ADC-A (Capacitance Probe Voltage, not pictured), and then manually turned the micrometer stage across a ~1mm range. There's some error due to backlash in the stage, but it was more than sufficient for our needs.

We did two sets of 2 bidirectional sweeps, and the peak residuals were at a constant position in the micrometer stage, indicating it was a micrometer artifact, not capacitance probe. The slope was consistent and matched across directions and sweeps when accounting for backlash in the turning points.

We also wanted to find the placement of the voice coil and magnet that gave the most linear operating regime. The datasheet gave this graph, and its clear the most consistent [N/A] gain would be at its peak sensitivity. The test would be straightforward: shim the magnet towards the voice coil until peak sensitivity is measured. By inputting a constant Vin, peak sensitivity is when displcement via capacitance probe peaks (assuming resistance in Vsense stays constant across tests, which was believed true due to the downtime needed to reshim and shift the magent).

Calibration Attempt Results... After a few repeat measurements, it became very obvious manual alignment error in the voice coil and magnet's concentricity axis was much greater than its internal positioning in the stroke range.* The magnet and voice coil both used 10-32 bolts, and the mech block grid was designed around 1/4". That led to some placement variability, plus the voice coil being suspended on a cantilevered leaf spring flexure. (Maybe if we had more time, we could've 3D printed an alignment jig instead of doing it by feel) Since we were a little short on time, and also the actual indentation would rarely exceed 1mm, we moved on to the next step.

VCA Force is linear with Current, but we only have direct Voltage input, with Vsense as a reference voltage to calculate current. So using provided calibration masses, we wanted to validate the linearity with sustained use (heating in the resistor affecting reference Vsense), find the proportionality between Vin and Force, as well as gauge our flexure's stiffness.

We tried to keep the calibration setup as close to the final configuration as possible. We used the v-groove bearing as a pulley, and tried to keep the string's horizontal axis perfectly parallel with the capacitance probe. We probably could have calculated the exact offsets by geometry, but didn't feel it was needed based on the results (and we were a little short on time lol).

Soo the first tries were not great- the linearity should be consistent and bidirectional. There was some frictional effect that we couldn't figure out. It could be from the bearing, the string stretching, or even the design of the flexure not retaining perfect concentricity between the voice coil and magnet, or some other artifact in our design.

This was like 4am a few days before our flight, and we were SO close to giving up, until Justin saw this rat tail of a wire barely scraping the table. So we forcibly twisted it until it was floating, and not pulling on the voice coil in any significant direction, and we were able to get clean calibration results!

LabVIEW Controls

We basically went as simple as possible for the controls, having not much labVIEW experience. The state machine cycles through all steps needed for the Oliver-Pharr process automatically, and Vin was controlled using Vsense as current feedback, with Force estimated using the displacement (consistent with the known flexure stiffness until contact). An initial idle state estimates the rate of thermal drift throughout the indentation, as well as baseline noise. The free-air approach allows for active fitting of the flexure stiffness, and a contact detection of 3mN to begin the loading stage. Then a t_hold, unloading, t_drift at 10% Force, and then final unload. There was also an Abort state that would trigger upon error to prevent damage to the tip. If we had more time, we probably could've improved the contact detection, as it was a large source of error, especially at the 50 mN indentation. Some sort of signal processing for noise reduction probably would have been very helpful too, but we were a little short on time. There are lots of things from a controls perspective we could have improved and things to have focused on more, but there's always next year :)

Also, the LabVIEW setup did not come without casualties. We didn't realize it was uncompatible with Windows 11, so when I tried to reinstall it on my (brand new, 1 month old) laptop, it corrupted my operating system... And I had to wipe my laptop and reinstall Windows. That was not very fun.

This was resolved by using a VM that ran Windows 10. Except that also meant it was very, very slow. We also used TDMS file writeout to the myRIO, which saved the data in its internal storage, so we copied it over to the VM's local drive, and then uploaded to Google Drive, and then downloaded on the host pc to post process... Maybe there was a cleaner way, but it worked lol.

Testing

We don't have pictures of the substrate holder, but we did a few attempts using the dummy tip first to ensure the system was fully functional through the loading/unloading stages and errors triggered the abort state properly. We taped the Cu sample to the substrate holder going over the corners, since double sided tape under the material would create artificial compliance that wasn't from the Cu.

After verifying the system worked correctly through all the desired states, we decided to go ahead and install the Berkovich indenter. We honestly weren't super sure what to expect using a soft dummy indenter, and this seemed relatively reasonable? This also might've been the day before we left for Chicago, so there might have been a little time crunch.
